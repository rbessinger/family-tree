Stories from Philip and Mary Baratz: Barban to Baratz

Find on the treeAll stories

Sources: Mary Baratz's 1927 naturalization petition and the SS Lapland passenger manifest of October 1921 (records read in full during the October 16, 2026 research round), with family testimony from recorded calls with Marsha Siegel and Wendy Bessinger on October 16, 2026.

Pincus and Mirla

Philip Baratz was born Pincus Barban on November 5, 1883, in Poland (per Mary Baratz's 1927 naturalization petition). Mary Baratz was born Mirla Barban on July 20, 1890, in Kremnitz, Poland, the town also recorded as Krzemieniec or Kremenets, in historic Volhynia, today western Ukraine (per her 1927 naturalization petition). They married on January 25, 1908, in Poland (per the same petition). Marsha Siegel, their granddaughter, knew her grandmother's maiden name not at all; the records below are how it came back.

He came first

Philip crossed first, alone, sometime before 1921; the exact ship and date of his own arrival are still open. He was naturalized on March 4, 1926, under the name Pincus Barban (per Mary's 1927 naturalization petition, which records his naturalization under that name). Only afterward did the family name in America become Baratz. Nobody at Ellis Island changed it; like so many of these changes, it was made later, in America, by the family itself.

The Lapland crossing

Mary followed with the children in the fall of 1921. Per her 1927 naturalization petition, she emigrated from Antwerp, Belgium, about October 12, 1921, and arrived in New York on October 22, 1921, aboard the SS Lapland, traveling under the name Mirla Barban. The passenger manifest (SS Lapland, sailed Antwerp October 13, 1921, List 5, lines 26 to 28) lists Mirla Barban, 31, wife; Bluma Barban, 12, scholar; and Perce Barban, 9, child; nationality Russian, race Hebrew. Bluma became Bess (Bessie); Perce became Peter. The manifest gives their last residence in a spelling that reads "Siemienic," very likely a misreading of Krzemieniec, and names the nearest relative left behind: "Mother F. Fingerit, Krzemieniec."

Fingerit: a likely maiden name, held for confirmation

That single manifest line is the best evidence of Mary's family name. If the mother left behind in Krzemieniec was F. Fingerit, then Mirla was very likely born Mirla Fingerit. This rests on the manifest entry alone and is marked here as a strong lead, not a settled fact; it is held for family confirmation (Barby Baratz is the family's hope) and for any Krzemieniec record that surfaces. Nothing in the tree depends on it yet.

Mary signs her new name

In 1927, living at 1827 South Hamlin Avenue in Chicago, Mary petitioned for naturalization in the U.S. District Court in Chicago (petition no. 45089). The petition styles her "Mrs. Mary Baratz, formerly Mirla Barban," which is the family's name change caught in a single line of handwriting: Barban in the old country and on the ship, Baratz in Chicago. Their youngest child, Ida, was born in Chicago around 1925, an American from the start. The 1930 census shows the household in Chicago: Philip, Mary, Bessie, Peter, and little Ida, plus a boarder.
